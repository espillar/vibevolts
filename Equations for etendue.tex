\section{Symbols}
The units are a bit of a mess here; to make the equations a bit more 
transparent we work in photons, seconds, square meters, and 
steradians, so conversions from magnitudes will be necessary in 
places given common practice.
\newpage
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha$ is the brightness of the target
    being sought in units commensurate with
    $\mathrm{photons/second/meter^2}.$
    \item $\beta$ is the brightness of the sky
    ($\mathrm{photons/second/meter^2/steradian}$).
    \item $\gamma$ will be the SNR ratio.
    \item $\Omega$ is the area of the  FOV of the sensor/optical system ($\mathrm{steradians^2}$).
    \item $\omega$ is the area of the  FOV of a pixel ($\mathrm{steradians^2}$) or the seeing disk or the diffraction disk,
    whichever is largest. Basically this is the area over which
    the light is spread and needs to compete with the
    background. 
    \item $A$ is the effective collecting area 
    ($\mathrm{meter^2}$).
    \item $T$ is the integration time (seconds.) This may be the result
    	of processing $n$ shorter integrations $t.$ 
     \item $g$ is used in some places as the limiting flux 
   	in 	$\mathrm{photons/second/meter^2}.$
   	\item $\eta$ is the net quantum efficiency of the whole system
	\item $f$ is the fraction of light from an object captured
			in the photometry aperture
	\item $C$ is the calibration which gives the number of photons per second per square meter for a 0 magnitude object for the band being used.
\end{itemize}

\section{Derivation of Search Rate}

The signal from an object is 
$S \propto \alpha A T \eta f.$
Assuming we are background limited, the noise is
$N \propto \sqrt{\beta A T \eta \omega}. $
Putting things together

\begin{equation} 
SNR^2 = \gamma^2 =
{\alpha^2 A^2 T^2 \eta^2 f^2\over 
 \beta A T \eta \omega} 
\end{equation}


\begin{equation} 
\gamma^2 = 
{\alpha^2 A T \eta f^2\over 
 \beta    \omega} 
\end{equation}
so  

\begin{equation} 
\boxed{
T =
\frac{\gamma^2 \beta \omega}{\alpha^2 A  \eta f^2}  }
\end{equation}

This the ``dwell time" required to reach the given SNR for the given
object (assuming BLIP).

To get the search rate,  multiply by the total area surveyed:


\begin{equation}	\boxed{
	SR = \frac{\Omega}{T}= 
 \propto 
\left(\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)^2 
 \frac{A  \Omega}{\omega} { \eta f^2  \over  \beta  }}
\end{equation}
which has units commensurate with steradians/second.  

\section{Conventional Units: Magnitudes}
Typically, we will be numbers in magnitudes in a selected band;
this will be magnitude for a source or magnitude per square arc
second for a background number. 
For each band there is a calibration $C_b$ which gives the number
of photons per second per square meter for the filter band $b$ in 
question so the flux $f$ is given in terms of the magnitude $m$

\begin{equation}
	m = -2.5 \log_{10}(f/C_b)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
f = C_b 10^{-0.4 m}
\end{equation}

\section{Should a Volume Search Sensor be
Near or Far to Targets?}
Thinking naively, if you are further away
from targets, you will be able to cover more area or volume much faster, so perhaps that's advantageous?   
There are some subtleties here, but lets assume that the limiting factor is reaching the faintest outer shell of the volume to be surveiled.  Fixing the scanning of this shell as a requirement, and fixing the FOV of the telescope, the area of the FOV projected onto the shell 
$ A_{FOV} \propto d^2;$ so assuming a fixed dwell time the search rate also goes as $d^2.$  But the dwell time
needs to increase since sources will appear fainter.
The flux from the target 
$f \propto 1/d^2.$ Assuming BLIP once again, the required
integration time, or required dwell time, goes as $t \propto d^{-4}.$  Hence our search rate goes as 
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{search\ rate} \propto { 1\over d^2}
\end{equation}
It's best to place the sensor close to the surveilled volume.
